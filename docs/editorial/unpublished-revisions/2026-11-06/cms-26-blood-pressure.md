**Blood pressure is recorded as two numbers, measured in millimeters of mercury (mm Hg): systolic pressure when the heart pumps and diastolic pressure between beats.** For most nonpregnant adults, the American Heart Association's 2025 categories still place readings below 120/80 in the normal range, 120–129 with a diastolic value below 80 in the elevated range, 130–139 **or** 80–89 in Stage 1 hypertension, and 140 **or** 90 and higher in Stage 2. These are categories for interpreting measurements, not a diagnosis from one test. Try the [Blood Pressure Checker](/tools/blood-pressure-checker), then use the explanations below to decide what to discuss with your healthcare professional.

The number to pay attention to is whichever places the reading in the **higher category**. For example, 118/84 belongs in Stage 1 because the diastolic value is 84 despite the systolic value being below 120. The [2025 AHA/ACC high blood pressure guideline](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2025-high-blood-pressure-guideline/top-things-to-know) reinforces accurate measurement, an overall-risk assessment, and decisions based on repeated, properly obtained readings. The website's result does not calculate your risk of heart attack or stroke, establish a condition, or determine a safe treatment plan.

## What do systolic and diastolic pressure actually mean?

The first number is systolic pressure, the force exerted by circulating blood against artery walls when the heart contracts. The second number is diastolic pressure, measured while the heart relaxes between beats. Both provide useful clinical information. A value of 118/76 mm Hg means 118 mm Hg systolic and 76 mm Hg diastolic; these numbers describe pressure, not the number of heartbeats per minute. A [Resting Heart Rate Checker](/tools/resting-heart-rate-checker) estimates a different concept and should not be substituted for a blood-pressure monitor.

Persistent high pressure can affect blood vessels, the heart, brain and kidneys over time, even when you feel well. However, the reading changes with stress, recent movement, caffeine, cuff fit and the time of day. That is why the [AHA's explanation of blood-pressure readings](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings) recommends understanding the category and working with a medical professional rather than relying on a single number or an app's confidence-inspiring label.

## The 2025 AHA blood-pressure categories at a glance

The 2025 guideline maintains a four-part classification for most adults. **Normal** requires both values under the threshold. **Elevated** requires systolic 120–129 and diastolic below 80. **Stage 1** and **Stage 2** are triggered if either pressure value enters that level; you do not need both numbers to be elevated. The chart below summarizes the [2025 guideline](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2025-high-blood-pressure-guideline/top-things-to-know) and [AHA patient classification](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings).

**AHA 2025 categories for most nonpregnant adults; measured averages, symptoms and clinical circumstances matter**

| Category | Systolic (top) | Diastolic (bottom) | How to interpret |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | <120 | AND <80 | Both values must be below their thresholds |
| Elevated | 120–129 | AND <80 | Systolic elevated; diastolic still below 80 |
| Stage 1 hypertension | 130–139 | OR 80–89 | Either value qualifies when Stage 2 criteria do not apply |
| Stage 2 hypertension | ≥140 | OR ≥90 | Either value qualifies; prompt clinician follow-up |
| Severe hypertension | Higher than 180 | AND/OR higher than 120 | Urgent assessment, with emergency care if acute warning symptoms |

These categories do not automatically tell you whether you should take medication, how soon treatment should begin or which personal target is appropriate. A physician or other clinician evaluates averages, history, age, diabetes, kidney disease, vascular conditions and possible measurement errors. Pregnant and postpartum patients require special assessment; the adult table is not an obstetric triage tool.

## How to classify common readings without mixing up AND and OR

Consider a set of hypothetical, properly measured adult readings: **118/76** is normal; **126/78** is elevated; **118/84** is Stage 1 because the lower number crosses 80; **136/72** is Stage 1 due to systolic pressure; **142/78** is Stage 2 despite diastolic being normal; and **128/94** is Stage 2 because the diastolic pressure is at least 90. This is classification practice, not a list of diagnoses.

**Examples show the higher-category rule; repeat measurements before clinical decisions**

| Example | Category | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 118/76 mm Hg | Normal | Both values below 120/80 |
| 126/78 mm Hg | Elevated | Systolic 120–129 and diastolic under 80 |
| 118/84 mm Hg | Stage 1 | Diastolic in the 80–89 range |
| 136/72 mm Hg | Stage 1 | Systolic in the 130–139 range |
| 142/78 mm Hg | Stage 2 | Systolic at least 140 |
| 128/94 mm Hg | Stage 2 | Diastolic at least 90 |

A category is not a permanent identity. An unexpectedly high value can reflect incorrect technique or an acute situation, whereas a series of consistently high averages can require treatment even without symptoms. If you also use an educational [Heart Disease Risk Calculator](/tools/heart-disease-risk-calculator), do not mistake its points for the AHA PREVENT predicted 10-year cardiovascular risk; our [heart-risk interpretation guide](/wellness-hub/what-does-your-heart-disease-risk-score-mean) explains that distinction.

## How to measure blood pressure reliably at home

Prefer a **validated automatic upper-arm cuff** that fits your arm circumference. Wrist or finger devices are often less dependable. Bring your monitor to a healthcare visit when possible so its readings and your technique can be checked. The [AHA home monitoring instructions](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings/monitoring-your-blood-pressure-at-home) recommend avoiding tobacco, caffeinated drinks and exercise for about 30 minutes beforehand, emptying your bladder and resting quietly for at least five minutes before measurement.

Sit with your back supported and feet flat on the floor. Keep your legs uncrossed, support your arm at heart level, use the cuff on bare skin and avoid talking, texting or watching emotionally stimulating content during the measurement. Take two readings about one minute apart, note the date and time, and follow the measurement plan agreed with your care team. A cuff that is too small can give a falsely high number; a poor fit can distort either result. Do not compare readings from different devices as if they were automatically equivalent.

If your clinician asks for a home log, a common guideline approach is two readings in the morning and two in the evening for several days, with about seven days preferred and three a minimum when feasible. Record all values rather than selecting the lowest, and calculate the mean with your clinician. Measurement and diagnosis require professional interpretation. The [AHA guideline's monitoring emphasis](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2025-high-blood-pressure-guideline/top-things-to-know) also notes that cuffless smartwatches should not be relied on for accurate blood-pressure measurement at this time.

## Why a single high reading usually is not a diagnosis

Pressure is dynamic. Sleep disruption, activity, pain, stress, a full bladder, recent caffeine, incorrect posture and timing around medications may change a measurement. A single office visit can also differ from home readings; some people have higher readings in clinical settings, while others have higher values outside the office. Your clinician may ask for an appropriately collected home log or ambulatory monitoring to clarify the pattern.

If your reading is higher than usual but you feel well and it is **not** in the severe range, sit quietly, review your technique and remeasure according to your care plan. Do not stop or double prescriptions because of one value. The [AHA home monitoring guide](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings/monitoring-your-blood-pressure-at-home) explains how to record readings and when to contact your healthcare team. If you also track exercise, our [resting-heart-rate guide](/wellness-hub/normal-resting-heart-rate-by-age) can help interpret pulse data—while keeping pulse and blood pressure as separate measurements.

## What the 2025 guideline says about lifestyle changes and medication

For adults with elevated pressure or hypertension, lifestyle measures can reduce risk: a heart-healthy dietary pattern such as DASH, less sodium when appropriate, regular activity, healthy sleep and weight management, stress management, and limiting alcohol. The 2025 guideline supports these steps widely. But lifestyle advice must not be portrayed as a guaranteed substitute for medication; the decision depends on the average pressure and cardiovascular or kidney risk.

According to the [2025 AHA/ACC guideline summary](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2025-high-blood-pressure-guideline/top-things-to-know), medication plus lifestyle treatment is recommended for most adults with confirmed average blood pressure at or above 140/90. At confirmed averages of 130/80 or above, people with clinical cardiovascular disease, previous stroke, diabetes, chronic kidney disease or elevated estimated 10-year cardiovascular risk may also meet treatment criteria. In lower-risk adults at that level, the guideline recommends medication if pressure remains elevated after an initial three-to-six-month lifestyle trial. These are population-level recommendations, not a self-treatment algorithm.

The website's category cannot decide whether a medication should be started, stopped, intensified, or changed. Some medications are unsafe in pregnancy or require special monitoring. Ask a clinician to account for your complete health history. Do not interpret a metabolic age or 'biological age' calculator as a direct measurement of heart or artery aging: that would exceed the clinical evidence available from those educational tools.

## When a high reading needs urgent care

> **Possible hypertensive emergency: new serious symptoms take priority** — For a nonpregnant adult with blood pressure higher than 180 systolic and/or 120 diastolic **and** chest pain, shortness of breath, new weakness, numbness, trouble speaking, vision changes, severe concerning back pain or other signs of acute organ injury, **call emergency services immediately**. Do not wait for repeated home measurements or attempt rapid self-treatment. New stroke or heart-attack symptoms warrant emergency care even if the BP reading is lower.

A very high reading **without** new concerning symptoms is still serious, but it is not automatically a hypertensive emergency. The [AHA urgent blood-pressure advice](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings/when-to-call-911-for-high-blood-pressure) says that if you feel otherwise well and the value is above 180 and/or 120, wait at least one minute, measure again and contact your healthcare professional promptly if it remains very high. The 2025 guideline recommends timely assessment and treatment for severe hypertension without acute organ damage, generally in the outpatient setting. Avoid taking extra medication unless a clinician specifically directs it.

Do not use symptoms as a substitute for measurement or measurement as a substitute for evaluating symptoms. A person with chest pain, suddenly abnormal speech or trouble breathing should seek emergency attention regardless of whether a home cuff looks normal. If you are pregnant or recently gave birth, separate warning signs and lower thresholds may apply. ACOG's [preeclampsia and hypertension during pregnancy guidance](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/preeclampsia-and-high-blood-pressure-during-pregnancy) explains why pregnancy-related pressure changes require dedicated clinical care.

## What age, pregnancy and other conditions change

The adult category thresholds are commonly used across ages, but the safest **treatment target** is not always identical for everyone. Frailty, falls risk, limited life expectancy, kidney disease, heart failure and concurrent medications affect choices. A single consumer calculator cannot individualize those considerations, and it should not declare a higher reading 'normal for your age.' If readings are consistently elevated, your healthcare professional can recommend a personal goal and monitoring frequency.

Pregnancy and postpartum hypertension require special attention because preeclampsia and related conditions may develop even when someone has never had high blood pressure before. Severe headache, visual changes, upper abdominal pain, swelling accompanied by concerning symptoms, or shortness of breath deserve prompt clinical assessment; follow obstetric emergency instructions, not the generic adult wait-and-watch approach. See [ACOG's pregnancy hypertension FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/preeclampsia-and-high-blood-pressure-during-pregnancy). Children likewise use age, height and sex-specific assessment rather than this adult classification.

## A practical plan for your next medical visit

Record the time, blood-pressure values, pulse if the monitor gives it, medications, and the circumstances around unusually high or low readings. Bring the monitor and cuff information to your appointment. Ask whether your measurements are reliable, which averaging approach to use, whether any underlying conditions should be checked, and how urgently follow-up is needed. If you feel faint, have persistent dizziness, or develop worrying symptoms, tell your clinician even if the pressure is not classified as 'high.'

Use the [Blood Pressure Checker](/tools/blood-pressure-checker) as a category explainer, the [Heart Disease Risk Calculator](/tools/heart-disease-risk-calculator) as a separate educational checklist, and our [heart-risk score guide](/wellness-hub/what-does-your-heart-disease-risk-score-mean) to understand limitations. None replaces a qualified clinical risk assessment, a validated home monitor or a personalized treatment plan. The immediate purpose is to have more useful questions and better measurements—not to attach a diagnosis to a single reading.

**Bottom line:** In the 2025 AHA framework, higher systolic **or** higher diastolic numbers determine the adult category. Reliable repeated measurements and clinical risk determine what to do next. Severe readings with concerning symptoms warrant immediate emergency help; repeated severe readings without such symptoms still require prompt professional contact.

## People also ask

### Is 130/80 high blood pressure under the 2025 AHA guideline?

An average reading of 130 systolic or 80 diastolic can meet the Stage 1 category for most nonpregnant adults. A single measurement does not confirm the diagnosis. Review properly obtained readings and your clinical risk with a healthcare professional.

### Can my blood pressure be Stage 2 when only one number is high?

Yes. The more severe category applies when either number crosses its cutoff. For example, 142/76 is Stage 2 based on the systolic value, while 126/94 is Stage 2 based on the diastolic value. Confirm the pattern rather than making decisions from one reading.

### What should I do if my monitor reads above 180/120?

If you have chest pain, shortness of breath, new neurologic symptoms, vision changes, or other concerning signs, call emergency services immediately. Without symptoms, sit quietly, repeat after at least a minute and contact your healthcare professional promptly if the reading remains that high. Do not self-adjust medications.

### What is the most accurate way to measure blood pressure at home?

Use a validated upper-arm monitor with a correctly sized cuff. Avoid smoking, exercise, and caffeine for 30 minutes beforehand, empty your bladder, rest five minutes, keep feet flat and your arm supported, and take two readings about a minute apart. Share a dated log with your clinician.

### Do older adults, pregnant people, or people with kidney disease use the same plan?

The usual adult category chart may help describe a number, but individual treatment targets and urgency depend on health conditions, frailty, pregnancy, medications and clinical risk. Pregnancy has separate hypertensive-disorder guidance; do not use this general adult tool to decide whether pregnancy symptoms are safe.

## Three key takeaways

- Use the higher category of either the systolic or diastolic number. Under AHA's 2025 framework, Stage 1 begins at 130 systolic or 80 diastolic.
- One reading does not establish hypertension. Accurate, repeated measurements and overall health risks guide clinical evaluation and treatment.
- If pressure is severely high with chest pain, breathing difficulty, vision changes or neurologic symptoms, seek emergency care. Severe readings without symptoms still require prompt clinician contact.
