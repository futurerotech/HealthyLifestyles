# Due Date by Conception Date: LMP, IVF, and Ultrasound

**A pregnancy due date is an estimate of when delivery may occur, not a deadline or a prediction of a particular birthday.** Obstetric care usually counts gestational age from the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP). In the standard model, that gives an estimated delivery date 280 days, or 40 weeks, later. If a conception date is genuinely known, the corresponding estimate is approximately 266 days after conception. The [Pregnancy Due Date Calculator](/tools/due-date-calculator) can show these conventions side by side, but your prenatal care team's documented date takes priority.

Different starting dates can lead to different answers. What matters for clinical decisions is the **best obstetric estimate**, established from menstrual history, appropriate early ultrasound, and, for assisted reproduction, the known embryo age and transfer date. ACOG, AIUM, and SMFM explain this approach in their [methods for estimating the due date](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2017/05/methods-for-estimating-the-due-date). A website cannot assess the quality of a scan, menstrual-cycle reliability, fetal growth, or an individual's pregnancy risk.

## What does a due date actually measure?

Gestational age is the number of completed weeks and days of pregnancy using the obstetric dating convention, not simply the time since fertilization. For someone who conceived around the middle of a 28-day cycle, gestational age is approximately two weeks ahead of the time since conception. That is why 'six weeks pregnant' does not mean conception happened six weeks ago. The underlying dates are used to plan prenatal visits, interpret fetal growth, and schedule certain screenings; they are not proof of the date fertilization occurred.

ACOG's [patient guide to pregnancies going past the due date](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/when-pregnancy-goes-past-your-due-date) explains why the estimated date helps track pregnancy progress while remaining an estimate. A prenatal clinician may establish a working due date early and avoid changing it with every later scan. When you want an everyday summary of your recorded pregnancy stage, the [Pregnancy Week Calculator](/tools/pregnancy-week-calculator) and our [week-by-week pregnancy guide](/wellness-hub/pregnancy-week-by-week-guide) can supplement, but not replace, your prenatal schedule.

## How do the three calculation methods compare?

The calculator offers three starting points: last menstrual period, conception date, and IVF embryo transfer. These are alternatives for interpreting dates, not three independent medical diagnoses. An LMP-based result is most straightforward for someone with a confidently known period date and a fairly predictable cycle. A conception-based result assumes the conception date is known; an IVF estimate uses the documented transfer date and the embryo's age, which your fertility clinic can verify.

**Equivalent estimated delivery-date conventions; actual prenatal dating should follow your clinician's records**

| Starting point | Educational date rule | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First day of last menstrual period | LMP + 280 days (40 weeks) | Assumes conventional cycle timing; may be inaccurate with irregular cycles |
| Estimated conception date | Conception + 266 days (38 weeks) | The actual fertilization date is often uncertain |
| Day-3 IVF embryo transfer | Transfer + 263 days | Must know the embryo was at day 3 at transfer |
| Day-5 IVF embryo transfer | Transfer + 261 days | Must know the embryo was at day 5 at transfer |

The arithmetic comes from the convention that 40 weeks elapse from the LMP, whereas approximately 38 weeks elapse from fertilization in a typical cycle. These rules are outlined in ACOG's [due-date dating opinion](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2017/05/methods-for-estimating-the-due-date). For irregular cycles, medications that alter bleeding patterns, recent pregnancy, or uncertain historical dates, an online rule may depart significantly from the clinical estimate.

## Why an estimated conception date may not equal the date of intercourse

People commonly use the dates of intercourse, an ovulation app's highlighted day, or a positive pregnancy test as a conception date. Those are different events. Sperm may remain capable of fertilization for several days in the reproductive tract, and ovulation can shift from cycle to cycle. Even when intercourse occurred on a remembered date, that does not precisely establish fertilization. Therefore, adding 266 days to a suspected conception date produces a calendar estimate with uncertainty that a calculator cannot remove.

A [cycle-based Ovulation Calculator](/tools/ovulation-calculator) illustrates timing assumptions rather than identifying the exact day an egg was fertilized. Our [fertile-window explanation](/wellness-hub/ovulation-calculator-fertile-window-guide) discusses why calendar estimates vary. Do not use either result to assign paternity, interpret a pregnancy test as a diagnostic timeline, or contradict an early ultrasound-based obstetric estimate.

## How IVF changes the due-date calculation

With in vitro fertilization, embryo age and transfer timing are documented by the fertility clinic. ACOG specifically recommends using assisted-reproduction-derived gestational age to assign the estimated due date. For an embryo transferred at five days of development, the usual date calculation is the transfer date plus 261 days. For an embryo transferred at three days, it is the transfer date plus 263 days. These offsets account for the embryo's developmental age and align the pregnancy with the conventional 40-week obstetric calendar.

A frozen embryo transfer does not mean that the weeks spent frozen are added to gestational age. The relevant age is the embryo's stage **at transfer**, plus the actual transfer date. If your clinic gives a transfer protocol or a definitive due date that differs from an app, ask the clinic to reconcile the records before scheduling screening or making delivery plans. A site calculator does not have access to laboratory documentation or treatment protocols. See the [ACOG assisted-reproduction guidance in its dating opinion](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2017/05/methods-for-estimating-the-due-date).

## When first-trimester ultrasound gives the better estimate

A high-quality first-trimester ultrasound is generally the most accurate clinical method to establish or confirm gestational age when IVF-derived dating does not apply. ACOG describes crown-rump-length dating up to 13 weeks and 6 days, with typical uncertainty of about five to seven days. This is not a promise that the scan identifies the conception date. Clinicians assess how well the ultrasound and menstrual dates agree, including the timing of the scan and the reliability of the LMP, before deciding whether to revise the estimated date.

For example, ACOG notes that when a scan before nine weeks differs by **more than five days** from LMP dating, revising the due date can be appropriate; later in the first trimester, the relevant cutoff may be greater. You should not apply a single internet rule yourself, because the gestational week and the quality of the scan matter. Its [clinical due-date recommendation](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2017/05/methods-for-estimating-the-due-date) covers these thresholds, while ACOG's [ultrasound exams FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/ultrasound-exams) explains what a standard examination can and cannot assess.

Later ultrasounds have wider dating uncertainty. If an early reliable estimated date has already been established, later measurements primarily help evaluate fetal growth, rather than resetting the clock every time fetal size differs from an average. If no suitable scan has established or revised the estimated delivery date before 22 weeks, the pregnancy may be considered suboptimally dated under ACOG guidance. This is a reason to discuss appropriate monitoring with your clinician, not to assume something is wrong.

## Worked examples: four dates, one estimate

Consider four independent example records, all yielding the same calendar estimate. A person whose last period began January 1, 2026 has an LMP-based result of October 8, 2026. Someone with a genuinely known conception date of January 15, 2026 also obtains October 8 when 266 days are added. An IVF transfer dated January 18 for a day-3 embryo adds 263 days; a day-5 embryo transferred January 20 adds 261 days. Both also yield October 8, 2026. These examples demonstrate the arithmetic, **not** equal clinical certainty across methods.

**Mathematically equivalent examples; not dates for actual medical scheduling**

| Example | Entered date | Calculation | Estimated due date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LMP | Jan 1, 2026 | +280 days | Oct 8, 2026 |
| Known conception | Jan 15, 2026 | +266 days | Oct 8, 2026 |
| Day-3 embryo transfer | Jan 18, 2026 | +263 days | Oct 8, 2026 |
| Day-5 embryo transfer | Jan 20, 2026 | +261 days | Oct 8, 2026 |

If your result differs from your ultrasound report, do not average the two dates or repeatedly choose whichever one looks more reassuring. Bring the LMP date, cycle information, scan dates and embryo transfer record (if applicable) to the obstetric team. The clinician should document the best estimate and explain any revisions. ACOG's [prenatal-care guidance](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/prenatal-care) recommends bringing menstrual-date information and a complete health history to your first prenatal visit.

## What if cycles are irregular, dates are uncertain, or results disagree?

Long or short cycles, recent contraception changes, breastfeeding, polycystic ovary syndrome, cycle-to-cycle variation, or simply not remembering the last period can weaken an LMP estimate. For example, someone who consistently ovulates substantially later than day 14 might appear farther along by LMP than an early scan suggests. A positive pregnancy test confirms a hormone response under the test's instructions, but does not by itself establish the gestational week. A mobile calendar is particularly uncertain if there were multiple bleeding episodes.

Ask your care team what date is actually recorded in your pregnancy chart and which evidence supported it. The best date may be based on early ultrasound, embryo transfer information, or consistent clinical records; your online result is only a starting point for questions. If you are planning nutrition or weight discussions, use the [Pregnancy Weight Gain Calculator](/tools/pregnancy-weight-gain-calculator) as general context, but do not tie diet or monitoring decisions to an unconfirmed calendar week.

## Prenatal care, safety signs, and when to seek help

If you think you are pregnant, arrange prenatal care rather than waiting until the app's estimated gestational week reaches a chosen threshold. Early appointments give your clinician an opportunity to confirm dating, review medications and health conditions, and plan appropriate screening. ACOG's [prenatal-care FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/prenatal-care) describes what information to bring and why visit timing can differ between patients.

> **Urgent symptoms cannot be evaluated by a due-date calculator** — Seek urgent medical care for heavy vaginal bleeding, severe or persistent abdominal pain, fainting, a severe headache that will not resolve, vision changes, trouble breathing, or fluid leakage during pregnancy. If you have severe symptoms, contact emergency services promptly. A calculated due date neither rules out ectopic pregnancy nor assesses fetal wellbeing. The CDC's urgent maternal warning-sign list provides more examples: https://www.cdc.gov/hearher/maternal-warning-signs/

If bleeding or pain occurs early in pregnancy, a home calculator cannot distinguish normal variation from an urgent condition. Later in pregnancy, a meaningful change in fetal movement or symptoms that concern you should be discussed promptly with your maternity team. The [CDC urgent maternal warning signs](https://www.cdc.gov/hearher/maternal-warning-signs/) apply even after delivery. The safest interpretation of an estimated date is as a planning aid alongside clinical evaluation, not as reassurance in the presence of symptoms.

**Bottom line:** LMP +280 days, conception +266 days, and day-3/day-5 embryo-transfer offsets are transparent arithmetic. Their usefulness depends on the reliability of the input. For prenatal decisions, rely on the best obstetric estimate recorded by your clinician, especially when early ultrasound or IVF records give more reliable dating information.

## People also ask

### Is a due date counted from the day I conceived?

Usually, prenatal gestational age is counted from the first day of the last menstrual period, about two weeks before ovulation in the standard 28-day-cycle model. A conception-based estimate adds about 266 days; your obstetric clinician can determine which date should be documented after reviewing history and ultrasound.

### Why did an early ultrasound change my due date?

First-trimester ultrasound uses embryo measurements to estimate gestational age, often more accurately than an uncertain menstrual date. A clinician may revise the working due date when the difference meets accepted dating criteria. Later scans usually track growth rather than continually redate a reliably dated pregnancy.

### How do I calculate an IVF due date?

Use the actual embryo-transfer date and embryo age, not an estimated intercourse date. A day-5 transfer is generally transfer date plus 261 days; a day-3 transfer is plus 263 days. Confirm the documented obstetric due date with your fertility or prenatal team.

### Does a late or irregular period make the calculator wrong?

It can make an LMP-only calculation unreliable because ovulation does not always occur on cycle day 14. Tracking bleeding, apps, and symptoms cannot pinpoint fertilization with certainty. Provide your clinician with whatever dates you know and ask how ultrasound findings should be incorporated.

### Can I use an online due date to schedule tests or decide whether pregnancy is overdue?

No. Use the gestational age and estimated due date recorded by your prenatal clinician for screening appointments, growth assessment, and delivery decisions. Contact the care team for symptoms, unusual bleeding or fluid leakage, or if the online calculation conflicts with your medical record.

## Three key takeaways

- Due dates are estimates: LMP plus 280 days, conception plus about 266 days, or embryo-transfer-based IVF offsets are calendar conventions rather than certain birth dates.
- First-trimester ultrasound is generally best for clinical dating when assisted reproduction does not apply; your care team documents the final obstetric estimate.
- Do not use a calculator to schedule prenatal tests, rule out complications, or interpret concerning pregnancy symptoms. Seek clinical care for warning signs.
